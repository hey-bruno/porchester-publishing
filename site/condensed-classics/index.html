The Condensed Classics

A gateway drug to the classics.

You know who you are. You sort of think you should read the classics, but the unread books have become an important chore you keep putting off. The Condensed Classics gets readers with literary ambitions and the attention span of a fruit fly through the story, in the language they came to read.

Fat Free and Semi-Skimmed give you the real words, fewer of them, with new illustrated cheat sheets to keep you oriented. Call it literary sacrilege. Finishing the book is a useful place to start.

Explore the collection ↓

Getting hooked

Someone tells you about a book. You watch the adaptation, read a plot summary or end up on Wikipedia. Curiosity has already done some of the work. A condensed edition brings you into the prose itself: the dialogue, humour, ideas and turns of phrase that a synopsis cannot give you.

Start with either cut. If Fat Free gets you hooked, Semi-Skimmed gives you more; if you love it, read the freaking original. You can also finish a condensed edition and leave it there. Nobody has to earn permission to enjoy a book.

Switch as you read

The combined ebook links the two cuts at corresponding passages. You can move from Fat Free to Semi-Skimmed halfway through, read a scene at greater length, or switch back when you want to pick up the pace.

Fat Free and Semi-Skimmed also come as standalone editions. The milk names describe the cuts, not percentages of retained text.

Leo Tolstoy

Love, family ambition and Napoleon’s invasion of Russia, in Tolstoy’s prose through a classic English translation. Relationship diagrams keep the families straight, while maps and timelines help you follow the war.

Where the cheat sheets came from

“When I was at school, I made cheat sheets for revision. They held very condensed information: what was happening, when it happened, dates and whatever else helped me remember it quickly. The point was to make a lot of material easier to grasp and retain. That is the thinking behind the guides in this collection.”

All killer, no filler.

We edit for momentum. Decisions, discoveries, reversals and consequences guide the selection, together with the character changes and essential ideas that give them meaning. Description and the material between those moments receive a harder cut. The result follows the story from beginning to end, ending included.

Cutting changes the experience. Atmosphere, accumulated detail and secondary lives lose space; words can carry a different weight when their surroundings disappear. Lovers of the original have reasons to object. These editions offer a pragmatic way through books that otherwise stay unread, with enough of their language to get a reader interested in more.

Cheat sheets for the reading

The new guides are part of the condensation. A family tree holds relationships in view; a timeline puts dates and events in order; a map locates the action. Compact explanations and visual representations of ideas help you understand and remember important information, including essential context from passages cut for length.

Each guide answers a question the reading raises, where you need the answer, without giving away what happens next. It has an accessible text equivalent. A diagram can carry a relationship or a sequence; the major dramatic scenes still belong in the prose. Guide reading and viewing time is included in the estimates.

A separate “What this cut leaves out” overview is the next extension of this approach: an optional, spoiler-labelled account of substantial omissions and what they contributed. It explains the losses rather than summarising every deletion. That overview is not yet part of the War and Peace pilot.

The words and the evidence

99.85%

In the Fat Free War and Peace pilot, 99.85% of the narrative wording is verbatim from Louise and Aylmer Maude’s translation: 40,965 source words and 61 introduced or altered words. This measures where the words come from, not accuracy or completeness. It excludes headings, guides, front and back matter, diagram labels and the source footnote.

We try to add nothing to the narrative. You read the author’s prose in the original English or a credited classic translation. Essential additions or alterations appear in brown square brackets. New headings, cheat sheets, diagrams and guide explanations are editorial material, also brown and clearly separate from the source prose.

See how unevenly the cuts fall

The cut retains 7.27% of all indexed source words, including source footnotes. Across seventeen divisions, retention ranges from 4.42% to 15.10%. Of 365 chapters, 155 contribute no wording, 28 retain at least a quarter and three retain more than half.

These figures demonstrate uneven selection. Establishing how much more description is cut than plot requires a separate classification of the source passages. That editorial priority has not yet been quantified by narrative function. The figures describe this pilot, not every title; they do not establish reading pace or fidelity.
