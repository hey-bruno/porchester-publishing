The Condensed Classics

A gateway drug to the classics.

You know who you are. You sort of think you should read the classics, but the unread books have become an important chore you keep putting off. The Condensed Classics gets readers with literary ambitions and the attention span of a fruit fly through the story, in the language they came to read.

Fat Free and Semi-Skimmed give you the real words, fewer of them, with new illustrated cheat sheets to keep you oriented. Call it literary sacrilege. Finishing the book is a useful place to start.

Explore the collection ↓

Getting hooked

Someone tells you about a book. You watch the adaptation, read a plot summary or end up on Wikipedia. Curiosity has already done some of the work. A condensed edition brings you into the prose itself: the dialogue, humour, ideas and turns of phrase that a synopsis cannot give you.

Start with either cut. If Fat Free gets you hooked, Semi-Skimmed gives you more; if you love it, read the freaking original. You can also finish a condensed edition and leave it there. Nobody has to earn permission to enjoy a book.

Read more

Want to explore what was left out? At the end, Read more gathers a named selection of episodes and passages that were cut or shortened. Each is labelled as an expanded passage from Semi-Skimmed or a passage from the original, with a link to read it and a clear way back. Where the format supports it, you can carry on in the fuller edition from there. It is a selection, not a list of every cut, and Fat Free on its own is a complete read.

Fat Free and Semi-Skimmed come together in one edition, linked so you can move between them. The milk names describe the cuts, not percentages of retained text. Reading times include the main text, guides, check-ins and glossary; optional extra reading adds time.

All killer, no filler.

We edit for momentum. Decisions, discoveries, reversals and consequences guide the selection, together with the character changes and essential ideas that give them meaning. Description and the material between those moments receive a harder cut. The result follows the story from beginning to end, ending included.

Cutting changes the experience. Atmosphere, accumulated detail and secondary lives lose space; words can carry a different weight when their surroundings disappear. Lovers of the original have reasons to object. These editions offer a pragmatic way through books that otherwise stay unread, with enough of their language to get a reader interested in more.

Cheat sheets for the reading

The new guides are part of the condensation. A family tree holds relationships in view; a timeline puts dates and events in order; a map locates the action. Compact explanations and visual representations of ideas help you understand and remember important information, including essential context from passages cut for length.

Each guide answers a question the reading raises, where you need the answer, without giving away what happens next. It has an accessible text equivalent. A diagram can carry a relationship or a sequence; the major dramatic scenes still belong in the prose. Guide reading and viewing time is included in the estimates.

Check-ins

Minute 30. Minute 60. Minute 90. Minute 120. At each marker, a check-in picks up what has happened, where you are in the story or argument, which characters, groups or voices matter, and what you now know that helps you carry on. Read straight through or across several sittings: after a few days away from the book, you still have the thread.

Check-ins only cover what you have already read, so they never give anything away. In the Portuguese editions they keep their own titles: Minuto 30, Minuto 60, and so on.

A glossary you will actually use

Unfamiliar words, the other names characters go by, mythological references and who does what: the glossary lets you look something up without losing the thread. Entries run in alphabetical order, one term each, with a short definition set apart from the keyword, room between entries and cross-references where they help. No wall of small print.

The words and the evidence

99.85%

In the Fat Free War and Peace pilot, 99.85% of the narrative wording is verbatim from Louise and Aylmer Maude’s translation: 40,965 source words and 61 introduced or altered words. This measures where the words come from, not accuracy or completeness. It excludes headings, guides, front and back matter, diagram labels and the source footnote.

We try to add nothing to the narrative. You read the author’s prose in the original language or a credited classic translation. Essential additions or alterations are clearly marked. New headings, cheat sheets, diagrams, check-ins, glossary entries and guide explanations are editorial material, clearly separate from the source prose.

See how unevenly the cuts fall

The cut retains 7.27% of all indexed source words, including source footnotes. Across seventeen divisions, retention ranges from 4.42% to 15.10%. Of 365 chapters, 155 contribute no wording, 28 retain at least a quarter and three retain more than half.

These figures demonstrate uneven selection. Establishing how much more description is cut than plot requires a separate classification of the source passages. That editorial priority has not yet been quantified by narrative function. The figures describe this pilot, not every title; they do not establish reading pace or fidelity.

Questions and answers

Who asked for this?

I did, years before I could do anything about it. At school and at university we were always handed excerpts from the classics: a chapter photocopied here, a passage from the library there. You never felt you had actually got through a book that way. The complete books were always there, free at the library, and they still are. But most people don’t read much, and most of those who do don’t read these books.

Plenty of people, me included, feel that a well-rounded person should have read certain classics. The problem is that they’re intimidating, mostly because of their length, and sometimes because of language with a reputation for being impenetrable. With so many options and so little time, the wish to read one stays dormant for years. If you catch a glimpse of the story at all, it’s usually through a film or series. That’s a fine introduction, but a condensed edition gives you the author’s own words, from beginning to middle to end.

These editions are built for a fragmented day. The text comes in chunks you can read in about 30 minutes, the way you’d watch an episode of a TV series, and the recaps, diagrams and cheat sheets keep you oriented between sittings. If it works, it’s a gateway drug: to the full book, or to more by the same author.

How is this different from reading a summary?

A summary is useful. It condenses a book into language written for the people who will read the summary. But you lose the author’s choice of words, or, in a translation, the translator’s reading of them. A summary tells you what happens; it doesn’t give you how the author told it. You might get a sense of what the language felt like, but that isn’t the same thing, and in many cases it takes away much of the enjoyment of what the author wrote.

A condensed edition keeps the author’s words as untouched as possible, in the order the author put them. We don’t rearrange anything; we only cut for pace. You still get a summary, in the recap at each 30-minute marker of what that section covered. But between those markers, you’re reading the book itself.

Aren’t you just training people to have shorter attention spans?

No. Their attention spans are already short, and short-form video and social media keep shortening them. That’s simply the reality, and I’m adapting to it. We’re trying to make long, complex works of art readable for an audience that has free little hits of dopamine available all day, so it’s an uphill battle.

Our assumption is that without books like these, a great many young people would never experience these works in their authors’ own words at all. That alone is worth the effort. And for some, it’s the start of a journey: to more of these books, to longer cuts, or to the full thing.

What’s the approach to deciding what stays and what goes?

We cut for pace. The model is a film editor cutting a trailer. A trailer contains only scenes from the film; there’s no frame in it that isn’t in the movie. But it isn’t a summary either. It’s cut to carry momentum and hook the viewer.

We see this collection the same way: as the trailer for the full book. The words are what the author published, or, for a translated work, the translation’s rendering of the author’s intent. A trailer is there to put people in seats for the film. These editions are there to get people reading the longer versions of these books.

Do you rewrite anything, or is every word the author’s?

We try very hard not to. Sometimes cutting a sentence or a paragraph leaves a seam that no longer makes grammatical sense, and it needs a word or two that aren’t the author’s. When that happens, we’re completely transparent about it: those words are clearly marked, and we explain where they are so the reader knows. In the first two books in the collection, more than 99% of the words are the author’s. The recaps, cheat sheets and other supporting material are new, written for the collection, and are clearly separate from the text.

Do you keep the ending?

This is where the trailer comparison ends: of course we keep it. You’ve got to have the ending. We want readers to finish feeling they’ve got the whole book: beginning, middle and end. Everything important stays in.

Why name the editions after milk?

Because everyone knows how milk works, even vegans. Skimmed milk is still milk; you’ve just taken the fat out. Saying the classics have fat in them is a provocation, and I know it’s sacrilege. But for the attention span of a modern reader, yes, they do.

How much fat is a harder question. I started by working backwards: trim very long books so people can get through them. But touching these classics at all is an exercise in heresy, and deciding exactly how much to cut was a judgment call too hard to make in the abstract. Milk grades solved that. Everyone knows what skimmed and semi-skimmed mean: no fat, a little fat. Full Fat is the original, so go and read the damn thing. I was surprised nobody had used the idea, and it amused me.

What matters most to me is telling readers how much time they’re signing up for. You can usually judge a book by its thickness, but font size and paper weight make that a poor guide. So we choose the time first and make the edition fit it, diagrams and all. For shorter classics, Fat Free takes two hours and Semi-Skimmed four; for longer ones, four and eight. The text comes in sections of about 30 minutes, so the shortest editions take four sittings.

How do I choose between Fat Free and Semi-Skimmed?

You don’t have to. Every Fat Free edition comes with its Semi-Skimmed version, and in the ebook the two are linked, so you can move between them as you read. If you have the time, usually about double, and you want more of a passage, the fuller version is right there. Just pick up the Fat Free edition and decide as you go.

What are the cheat sheets, and aren’t they cheating?

Only if revising for an exam is cheating. When I was at school, I made cheat sheets before tests: who was related to whom, what was happening, what each character wanted. They were incredibly useful to me. I made them for myself; now I’m making them for anyone else who finds them useful.

In the books, they’re original editorial material by Porchester Publishing, placed alongside the author’s text and kept separate from it. They make the book easier to follow, remember and come back to, while you still read the author’s own language. They include:

  • Character guides: who people are, how they’re connected, whose side they’re on, and the different names one person goes by.
  • Maps: where things happen and where people go.
  • Timelines: what happened when, including flashbacks, parallel events and stories told within the story.
  • Diagrams and charts: how an argument, a conflict, a political structure or an unfamiliar idea works.
  • Short context notes: the history, culture or plot you need for a passage, including context from material that was cut.
  • Check-ins (Minute 30, Minute 60 and so on): what’s happened, where things stand, who matters and what you now know, so you can pick the book up again after a break.
  • A glossary: words, names, alternative names and references, alphabetical, with short definitions and cross-references.
  • Read more: at the end, a selection of named passages that were cut or shortened, with links to the fuller edition or the original and a way back.

They appear where they’re useful and never give away what comes later, and the visual guides have text versions. They don’t replace big scenes with summaries, rewrite the author in simpler language, or tell you what to think. Their reading time is included in the estimate for each edition; the optional extra passages add time.

These books are out of copyright. Why wouldn’t I just download a free copy?

By all means, download a free copy, or borrow the book from the library. What’s stopping you? If you’re happy reading the original, read the original; this isn’t for you. It’s for people who feel they should read these books but know themselves well enough to know they won’t finish. Call it a spoonful of sugar for the medicine.

Once you’ve finished the Fat Free edition, have you read War and Peace?

Yes. Nobody who asks “have you read War and Peace?” means only the unabridged edition. By that logic you’d have to have read it in Russian, and very few people have. The honest answer is yes, and if they ask which version, you tell them: Fat Free. You could be quizzed on what happens and answer with confidence. You could even quote it, because the words are Tolstoy’s, in a classic translation.
