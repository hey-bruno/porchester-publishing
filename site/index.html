São Paulo, October 1994. Two years after impeaching a president, Brazil buried Senna, won the World Cup, and changed its currency. Prices stopped going up, for now.

Odete is seventeen, has a black trench coat, and is scared she’ll fail the harsh entrance exam for her chosen university. She’s lived with her grandmother since her parents moved to a gated community outside the city without her. In the afternoons she minds her uncle’s rubber-stamp shop in a building officially called the Grand Galleria and known to everyone as the Rock Galleria, where São Paulo’s underground scene buys records, kills time, and turns every rumor into a conspiracy theory. Customers hardly ever show up, so she has time for what she does best: sticking her nose where it doesn’t belong and tracking every story to its source.

When the leader of a hardcore band shows up complaining that his demo tape has been tampered with, Odete decides to investigate. As the case gets hairier, she recruits a handful of people from the scene to chase it down: friends with more free time than qualifications, and a girl who complicates everything.

As the story gets out, the press blames the devil, extraterrestrials, a real-estate scam, or mass hysteria. A pastor goes on TV to call the building the maternity ward of the Apocalypse, and anyone who listens to metal, punk, or goth rock becomes a suspect.

With exams coming up and her family telling her to drop everything, Odete has to choose between the future expected of her and a mystery that was never any of her business.

You know who you are. You sort of think you should read 47 hours of a Russian novel, but you have the attention span of a fruit fly and it’s been on the shelf for years. Start with four hours of Tolstoy, in a classic English translation, and get through the whole story.

All killer, no filler. Fat Free has the pace of a movie trailer, using the original scenes and dialogue, and takes you through the entire story, ending included. You get Tolstoy’s language and humour as well as the plot, instead of the gist from Wikipedia or an AI summary. We try to add nothing to the prose.

Cutting Tolstoy down to four hours is literary sacrilege, but leaving him unread hasn’t done much for you either. If you like the writing and want more of it, switch mid-stream to Semi-Skimmed: the combined ebook links the corresponding passages. If you love it, read the freaking original.

Brand-new diagrams, graphs and illustrations

Family trees and relationship diagrams keep track of the cast. Maps and timelines help you follow the battles, with graphs to explain connections and ideas, and new illustrations throughout. The guides include accessible text equivalents, and their reading and viewing time counts towards the four hours.

How the reading times are calculated

Originals are estimated from the linked English editions at 200 words per minute, rounded to the nearest half hour. The condensed-edition estimates include time for the new visual guides. Percentages compare reading time with the original, not the percentage of words removed. Reading pace and translation affect the time needed.
